# Which companion perturbations are relevant?

14 September 2026. Follow-up to the reader's question about structural zeros.

## Input storage and backward error are different

The zeros and ones in the initial companion matrix can be represented exactly.
This alone does not constrain the effective backward error to the original
nonzero positions. QR transformations change coordinates and fill some original
zeros even in exact arithmetic. For example, for

\[
C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-b_0\\1&0&-b_1\\0&1&-b_2\end{pmatrix},
\quad Q=(e_2,e_3,e_1),
\quad R=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-b_1\\0&1&-b_2\\0&0&-b_0\end{pmatrix},
\]

we have the QR factorization \(C=QR\). One unshifted QR step gives

\[
RQ=Q^*CQ=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-b_1&1\\1&-b_2&0\\0&-b_0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

The entries at positions (1,2) and (2,2) need no longer vanish. Hessenberg
structure is preserved, but the original companion sparsity pattern is not.
Transporting rounding errors back through the transformations similarly need
not preserve that pattern. Full-matrix backward error is an equivalent-input
description, not a claim that the algorithm independently adds noise to every
stored zero.

## Algorithms can provide stronger guarantees

Aurentz–Mach–Robol–Vandebril–Watkins,
[*Fast and backward stable computation of roots of polynomials, Part II*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.02435)
(SIMAX 39(3), 1245–1269, 2018), Theorems 5.10–5.11, prove a coefficient
backward-error bound for their companion QR algorithm. Theorem 5.14 treats
scaled companion QZ. Their bounds retain a degree-dependent factor. These
results justify studying the original coefficient condition for these methods.
They do not require every intermediate matrix to have companion sparsity.

## An elementary structured condition calculation

For the monic polynomial \(q(x)=x^n+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_kx^k\), restrict
matrix perturbations to the last column:

\[
\delta C=-\delta b\,e_n^T,\qquad
\|\delta C\|_2=\|\delta C\|_F=\|\delta b\|_2.
\]

At a simple root, differentiation gives

\[
\delta z=-\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\delta b_kz^k}{q'(z)},
\qquad
\kappa_{\rm last\ column}(q,z)
=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|z|^{2k}}}{|q'(z)|}.
\]

This is exactly the fixed-leading-coefficient root condition. The draft's
original input allows the leading coefficient to vary too. Transferring that
input through \(b_k=a_k/a_n\) requires the norm induced by this map;
unweighted perturbations of \(b\) do not reproduce the original coefficient norm.

More generally, for a complex linear matrix perturbation space \(T\), measured
in Frobenius norm, the eigenvalue derivative has restricted norm

\[
\kappa_T(C,z)=\frac{\|\Pi_T(wr^*)\|_F}{|w^*r|}.
\]

Here \(\Pi_T\) is Frobenius-orthogonal projection. The formula follows from
\(w^*Er=\langle wr^*,E\rangle_F\). A sparsity mask gives one such projection;
perturbations of factors or other parameters can instead require a weighted
norm on their parameter space.

## Consequence for the project

The full-matrix overlap is a valid sensitivity benchmark when an eigensolver's
error is controlled only in an unstructured matrix norm. Its worst direction
need not be generated by a particular algorithm. The draft's relative-condition
ratio cannot be read as an unavoidable loss of accuracy from using companions.

The practical objective should specify the algorithm or its admissible
backward-error model, then analyze conditioning in that model. A useful next
comparison is coefficient-induced perturbations, a justified intermediate
structured model, and the full matrix space. The same issue is central to the
intended multivariate algebraic methods.
